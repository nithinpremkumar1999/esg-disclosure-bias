Separating efficiency from disclosure

How the treatment of missing data turns environmental tilts into size bets

1Why missing data matters

Environmental signals built from raw corporate disclosures, such as carbon, water and waste relative to the value a company creates, compared within sectors, are attractive because every number traces back to a filing rather than a modelled estimate. The cost is that every score depends on an earlier event: the company choosing to disclose.

Any strategy or index built on such a signal has to decide what to do with companies that report too little. A common and intuitive answer is to require a minimum level of disclosure, here at least two of carbon, water and waste, and to treat everyone else as the least efficient in their sector, so that non-disclosure counts as a red flag. Three developments make that choice more consequential:

  • Broader universes. Sustainable and factor indices increasingly run on investable-market universes that reach into small and mid caps, where disclosure is thinner.
  • CSRD Omnibus I. Mandatory sustainability reporting now applies only above 1,000 employees and €450m turnover, and the next wave of first reports moves to 2028 (Nexio, Dcycle).
  • Double materiality. Under the ESRS a company may omit water or waste as "not material". Two of the unscored companies in my sample, Latour and Adecco, do exactly that. Both are asset-light businesses, which an efficiency signal would usually favour.

Most portfolio construction handles size implicitly, through benchmark weights, rather than as an explicit exposure. A size tilt that arrives through data coverage can therefore pass unnoticed. The questions here are how large that tilt is, and whether a different treatment of absence avoids it without resorting to estimated data.

The key assumption is that not disclosing carries no information about true efficiency. If non-disclosers really are less efficient, part of the penalty's tilt is signal rather than bias. That is an empirical question, and anyone holding a history of disclosures can answer it (section 6).

4It holds whatever the calibration

Because the fitted slope is imprecise, I varied it from zero to twice its fitted value and repeated the exercise at higher unscored rates. With no size gradient the tilt is zero ({{ f.sweep_flat_T1 }}). It grows as the gradient steepens, and it scales with the share of companies left unscored: at the fitted slope, the underweight is {{ f.sweep_15_T1 }} with 15% unscored and {{ f.sweep_30_T1 }} with 30%. The neutral treatment stays within {{ f.sweep_T2_maxabs }} of zero across every case.

6What I would build

For a team running a disclosure-based signal, I would build three tools. None of them needs estimated or vendor data; they change only how absence is measured and treated.

  1. A missing-data audit. At each rebalance, split the active tilt into the part driven by scored efficiency and the part driven by unscored names, and report the size, sector and country profile of the unscored bucket. Then use the signal's own disclosure history to test whether non-disclosers turn out less efficient once they begin reporting. That test decides between a penalty and a neutral treatment on the team's own raw data.
  2. Disclosure status and dropout tracking. Classify every company as never reported, stopped reporting, declared not material, or reporting but stale. Omnibus I makes "stopped" a growing category and the ESRS make "not material" a new one, and each likely deserves different handling.
  3. A style decomposition of the signal. Attribute the signal's style exposures to size, value, sector and disclosure coverage, and track how the coverage share changes as investable universes broaden.

7Limitations

  • Sample size. 100 companies with 7 unscored, so the slope is imprecise. For {{ r.n_partial }} of the unscored companies, water or waste could only be checked against report summaries; counting them as scored makes the slope steeper but even less precise.
  • Disclosure flags. Flags use a first pass through Tracenable's extraction of company filings, with primary-source checks wherever scored status depended on it. A production signal's test of "sufficient" disclosure may be stricter.
  • Simulated scores. Efficiency scores are simulated and independent of size. Real scores may correlate with size; the oracle comparison isolates only the missing-data effect.
  • Size measure and universe. Size is proxied by ETF weight (free-float), and the universe is the STOXX Europe 600 only.
  • Omnibus flags. Flags are approximate: domicile is proxied by country of risk, thresholds were checked in detail only for borderline names, and non-EU companies with large EU turnover are not modelled.
  • Portfolio model. The tilt is a stylised linear, sector-neutral tilt (λ = {{ r.lambda }}), and tracking error uses a stylised risk model. Real portfolio construction differs.
