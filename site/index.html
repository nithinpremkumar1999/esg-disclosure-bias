Separating efficiency from disclosure

How missing data shapes the size exposure of a Resource Efficiency tilt

Nithin Premkumar · 9 October 2026

Independent analysis using public data and a stylised model. Not affiliated with, commissioned or reviewed by Osmosis Investment Management. It describes a mechanism worth measuring, not a finding about any Osmosis portfolio.

In brief

  • Disclosure thins out among smaller companies. In a 100-company sample of the STOXX Europe 600, 93 disclose enough to be scored, and the 7 that do not all sit in the smaller six size deciles. The size slope points the right way but is imprecise (p = 0.16).
  • Treating unscored as inefficient adds a quiet size bet. In a simulation where true efficiency is unrelated to size, the rule underweights the smaller half of the universe by 6.5% of its benchmark weight. Treating unscored companies as neutral removes that tilt and captures the same efficiency exposure (97.1% of the full-information maximum, against 97.2% under the current rule).
  • The bias grows where the market is heading. If Omnibus I ends disclosure for the roughly 24 EU and EEA constituents now below its thresholds, the underweight rises to 10.5%. In an illustrative universe with 30% unscored, closer to broad index universes, it reaches 25%.

1Why missing data matters

Osmosis builds its Resource Efficiency signal from what companies actually report: carbon, water and waste relative to the value they create, compared within sectors, from raw disclosures since 2005 and without third-party estimates (model overview). Every number traces back to a filing, which is the signal's strength. It also means every score depends on an earlier event: the company choosing to disclose.

A company is scored when it reports sufficiently on at least two of the three metrics, and the Core strategies treat companies without a score as inefficient (CIO note, September 2026). Three developments make that choice more consequential:

  • Broader universes. The 48 iSTOXX Osmosis Multi Factor Transition indices launched on 1 October run on STOXX investable-market universes, which reach further into smaller companies (launch note).
  • CSRD Omnibus I. Mandatory sustainability reporting now applies only above 1,000 employees and €450m turnover, and the next wave of first reports moves to 2028 (Nexio, Dcycle).
  • Double materiality. Under the ESRS a company may omit water or waste as "not material". Two of the unscored companies in my sample, Latour and Adecco, do exactly that. Both are asset-light businesses, which an efficiency signal would usually favour.

Size is handled in portfolio construction rather than as an exposure of its own (factor suite), so a size tilt that arrives through data coverage could pass unnoticed. Osmosis has already corrected one structural bias of this kind with the January 2026 Technology Hardware split (sector split). The question here is whether missing data is another.

2Who discloses

I drew 10 companies at random from each size decile of the 470 non-financial STOXX Europe 600 constituents (iShares holdings, 8 October 2026). For each, I recorded whether its latest report quantifies Scope 1 and 2 emissions, water and waste, with a source link for every flag, and applied the two-of-three rule.

93% of the sample is scored. The four largest deciles are fully scored; all 7 unscored companies sit in deciles 1 to 6. A logistic regression of scored status on log size gives a slope of 0.78 (95% CI -0.30 to 1.87). That implies a fitted probability of being scored of 86% in the smallest decile and 99% in the largest. The direction is consistent with the hypothesis, but seven events cannot pin down the slope, so section 4 tests a wide range around it.

3What the rule does to a portfolio

To isolate the mechanism, I simulated a sector-neutral Resource Efficiency tilt on the full 470-company universe 1,000 times. Each company gets a "true" efficiency score that is unrelated to size by construction, and each is scored with the probability fitted above. Every portfolio is compared with an oracle that sees every true score, so the difference measures only how absence is handled.

Under the current rule, the smaller half of the universe is underweighted by 6.5% of its benchmark weight (between 3.1% and 10.1% in 90% of runs). The weighted-average company in the portfolio is 2.8% larger than it would be with full information. Treating unscored companies as neutral removes the tilt (within ±1.7%) at no cost in efficiency captured. Size-neutralising the scores also removes it, but costs about 10% of the efficiency signal, a blunter tool.

Two further results matter. First, when disclosure is unrelated to size, the tilt disappears (+0.2%), so the cause is the size gradient in disclosure rather than the rule itself. Second, stylised tracking error barely moves (1.12% against 1.09%). The bias is quiet, which is why it is easy to miss.

The key assumption is that not disclosing carries no information about true efficiency. If non-disclosers really are less efficient, part of the current rule's tilt is signal rather than bias. That is an empirical question, and Osmosis's own history can answer it (section 6).

4It holds whatever the calibration

Because the fitted slope is imprecise, I varied it from zero to twice its fitted value and repeated the exercise at higher unscored rates. With no size gradient the tilt is zero (+0.2%). It grows as the gradient steepens, and it scales with the share of companies left unscored: at the fitted slope, the underweight is 13% with 15% unscored and 25% with 30%. The neutral treatment stays within 0.1% of zero across every case.

5Omnibus I is likely to make it larger

6 of the 74 EU and EEA companies in the sample fall below the new thresholds on average headcount or turnover: Gecina, Covivio, Gaztransport & Technigaz, CD Projekt, Frontline and Abivax. All of them sit in deciles 1 to 6, and Covivio is borderline at 959 average employees. Most of them report today; the question is whether they keep reporting once no longer required. Extrapolated by decile, about 24 constituents (5.1% of names, 1.3% of index weight) are exposed.

If a quarter, half or all of them stop disclosing, the current rule's smaller-half underweight rises from 6.5% to 7.5%, 8.5% and 10.5% (Chart 2). The neutral treatment stays near zero (0.1%).

6What I would build at Osmosis

None of this needs estimated or vendor data. It changes only how absence is measured and treated.

  1. A missing-data audit. At each rebalance, split the active Resource Efficiency tilt into the part driven by scored efficiency and the part driven by unscored names, and report the size, sector and country profile of the unscored bucket. Then use the history since 2005 to test whether non-disclosers turn out less efficient once they begin reporting. That test decides between the current rule and a neutral treatment on Osmosis's own raw data.
  2. Disclosure status and dropout tracking. Classify every company as never reported, stopped reporting, declared not material, or reporting but stale. Omnibus I makes "stopped" a growing category and the ESRS make "not material" a new one, and each likely deserves different handling.
  3. A style-drag decomposition. Attribute the raw factor's style drag to size, value, sector and disclosure coverage, and track how the coverage share changes as the iSTOXX universes broaden.

7Limitations

  • Sample size. 100 companies with 7 unscored, so the slope is imprecise. For 5 of the unscored companies, water or waste could only be checked against report summaries; counting them as scored makes the slope steeper but even less precise.
  • Disclosure flags. Flags use a first pass through Tracenable's extraction of company filings, with primary-source checks wherever scored status depended on it. Osmosis's own test of "sufficient" disclosure may be stricter.
  • Simulated scores. Efficiency scores are simulated and independent of size. Real scores may correlate with size; the oracle comparison isolates only the missing-data effect.
  • Size measure and universe. Size is proxied by ETF weight (free-float), and the universe is the STOXX Europe 600 only.
  • Omnibus flags. Flags are approximate: domicile is proxied by country of risk, thresholds were checked in detail only for borderline names, and non-EU companies with large EU turnover are not modelled.
  • Portfolio model. The tilt is a stylised linear, sector-neutral tilt (λ = 0.5), and tracking error uses a stylised risk model. Osmosis's portfolio construction differs.
